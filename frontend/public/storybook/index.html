PROLOGUE · A TRUE MISSION STORY

2012 — November 2026

The Universe in 102 Colours

✦

Light is a remarkable messenger. It can cross the darkness between stars, carry the chemical signature of a cloud, or arrive from a galaxy whose light began its journey billions of years ago. To read that message, humanity needed a different kind of map.

This is the story of SPHEREx: an observatory designed to survey the entire sky in near-infrared wavelengths. Its name is long, its telescope is modest, and its ambition is immense. Three questions brought its creators together: how did the universe begin, how did galaxies evolve, and where did the ingredients for planetary systems come from?

Follow the real mission from an unsuccessful proposal to orbit, through its first images and scientific results, and into a public archive that belongs to anyone who wants to look. The spacecraft is still working. This book ends at the latest documented milestone, not at the end of its mission.

MISSION RECORD

Three science goals. One sky. A planned two-year prime mission.

CHAPTER 01 · THE IDEA

2012–2019

Three questions, one sky

✦

The beginning was not a rocket. It was a disagreement with the limits of existing observations. In 2012, three mission concepts were being discussed: one to study the earliest expansion of the universe, one to investigate interstellar ices, and another to understand the evolution of galaxies.

Jamie Bock and the researchers behind these ideas saw that the same instrument could serve all three. Measure spectra over the celestial sphere, and a single observatory could connect the ancient universe with the material from which new stars and planetary systems emerge.

Their first attempt, in NASA’s Small Explorer competition, did not win selection. Instead of abandoning the idea, the team adapted it for the Medium Explorer competition. The new proposal was the product of thousands of pages of work. In February 2019, NASA selected SPHEREx. An idea that had once failed to secure a mission now had a path to space.

The scientific questions had not changed. What had changed was the way they could be asked: not by looking at one exceptional corner of the sky, but by surveying the whole of it.

MISSION RECORD

2019 selection: nine proposals had entered the 2016 competition; two concepts advanced to further study. NASA selected SPHEREx on February 13, 2019.

CHAPTER 02 · BUILDING THE EYE

2023–January 2025

The machine that had to be cold

✦

The difficult part of an infrared telescope is that everything warm gives off infrared radiation, including the telescope itself. Its own heat could drown out the faint light it was built to measure. The engineers therefore designed a spacecraft whose extraordinary shape was a solution to a problem, not an ornament.

Three nested photon shields would keep unwanted sunlight and heat away from the optics. A passive cooling system of V-groove radiators would direct heat outward into space. The telescope had to remain colder than about −210°C, while its sensitive detectors collected signals from across the sky.

A second challenge was colour. Instead of a large conventional spectrometer, the mission uses special filters over its detectors. Their transmission changes across the detector, so a sequence of differently pointed exposures builds a spectrum. Six detectors and their filters give the flight instrument its 102 nominal wavelength channels.

By late 2024 the observatory had undergone environmental testing at BAE Systems in Colorado. The machine that began as a proposal had become a real object: a telescope, shielded from its own warmth, ready to leave the world that built it.

MISSION RECORD

The flight instrument surveys roughly 0.75–5 micrometres. The 2019 selection announcement described an earlier 96-band design; 102 is the actual flight-mission specification.

CHAPTER 03 · LIFTOFF

March 11, 2025 · 8:10 p.m. PDT

The night the sky changed

✦

At Vandenberg Space Force Base on the California coast, a Falcon 9 rose from Space Launch Complex 4 East. Its engines lit the darkness on March 11, 2025. Inside the rocket’s fairing was SPHEREx, the culmination of years of proposals, tests and construction.

It was not travelling alone. Four small spacecraft from NASA’s PUNCH mission shared the launch. Their task was to investigate the Sun’s outer atmosphere and the formation of the solar wind. SPHEREx, meanwhile, was going to turn its attention outward to stars, galaxies and the vast sky beyond.

After the flight through the atmosphere, the upper stage released SPHEREx. The observatory entered a Sun-synchronous orbit about 650 kilometres above Earth. Its route around the planet was selected so the telescope could remain shielded from the Sun as it systematically scanned the celestial sphere.

The launch was a victory of engineering, but it was not yet a victory of astronomy. The instrument still had to cool, open, focus, and prove it could measure the light for which it had been built.

MISSION RECORD

Actual launch: March 11 in California; March 12 at 03:10 UTC. SPHEREx shared Falcon 9 with PUNCH.

CHAPTER 04 · OPENING ITS EYES

March 18–April 1, 2025

The first light

✦

For the first days in orbit, SPHEREx was a telescope that had not yet begun its survey. A protective cover had kept dust away from its optics during launch. On March 18, the mission team commanded the cover to be ejected. The opening was clear.

On March 27 the observatory captured some of its first images. The six detectors produced six views: thousands upon thousands of points of light, with different infrared wavelengths assigned visible colours by the science team. Across each detector image, colour revealed the design of the filters that would make the survey possible.

The photographs released on April 1 were not yet calibrated for scientific measurements. Their purpose was to show that the instrument was functioning as expected. The distinction mattered: a beautiful picture was only the beginning. Scientists still had to establish how its measured values related to the real brightness and wavelengths of objects in the sky.

The first images were a promise with evidence behind it. The telescope could see. Now it had to learn to measure.

MISSION RECORD

Six detector images per exposure. These first images were explicitly uncalibrated; they are not scientific measurements suitable for quantitative analysis.

CHAPTER 05 · THE LANGUAGE OF LIGHT

May 1, 2025

A cloud that vanished in another colour

✦

In April, the new telescope looked into the Large Magellanic Cloud, a neighbouring galaxy. At a wavelength of 3.29 micrometres, a cloud of carbon-rich dust glowed strongly. At 0.98 micrometres, the very same cloud was no longer visible in the image.

Nothing had disappeared from space. The difference came from the way the molecules emitted light. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, carbon-containing molecules, have distinctive infrared signatures. At the right wavelengths those signatures can be observed, while at other wavelengths they fade into the background.

This is the power of spectroscopy. Light is separated into its components, and its pattern becomes a clue to what an object is made of. For galaxies, spectral features can also help determine redshift, which in turn helps estimate cosmic distances.

On May 1, NASA announced that SPHEREx had begun routine science operations after weeks of preparation. The observatory was now taking about 600 exposures a day, with six detector images in each exposure. The great map was being assembled, not in one photograph, but in millions of measurements.

MISSION RECORD

About 3,600 detector images per day during survey operations. The dust-cloud example is a wavelength comparison, not a change in the cloud over time.

CHAPTER 06 · THE OPEN DOOR

July 2, 2025

The sky becomes a public record

✦

An observatory in orbit is difficult to reach. Its data need not be. On July 2, 2025, the mission began making its early survey observations available through the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive at Caltech.

Among the first released images was a view of the Vela Molecular Ridge. A region of glowing gas and dust, the emission nebula RCW 36, stood out in the composite. The image was made by combining selected infrared wavelengths into visible red, green and blue. Each underlying observation carried information that could be examined independently.

This public archive changed the audience for the mission. A researcher could download calibrated products and study them. A student could inspect a field of stars or compare observations. Different scientists could ask questions the mission’s original team had never anticipated.

The map was no longer just an instrument’s achievement. It had become a shared resource. The archive would continue to publish observations, improve their calibration and document the methods needed to interpret them.

MISSION RECORD

July 2, 2025: first public Quick Release data. IRSA began a weekly release cadence. A colour composite is a display product; scientific analysis should use calibrated data.

CHAPTER 07 · THE FIRST GREAT MAP

December 18, 2025

An entire sky, at last

✦

For months, SPHEREx returned to its orbit and its pattern of pointings. Each visit filled another part of the sky in another set of wavelengths. By December, the individual observations could be assembled into the first of the mission’s planned all-sky spectral maps.

NASA announced the milestone on December 18, 2025. The Milky Way ran across the centre of the released mosaic: a bright river of stars, hot gas and dust. Beyond its crowded plane were points of light from galaxies elsewhere in the cosmos.

The panorama was not a photograph of the universe from the outside. It was a projection of the sky as seen from our position within it, with selected infrared colours translated into visible ones. Behind the image lay a much larger collection of measurements in 102 nominal spectral channels.

For the science team, completing one survey was the beginning of a deeper investigation. Repeated surveys would improve coverage and sensitivity, and would also record the sky at different times. The universe, after all, is not a still picture.

MISSION RECORD

First all-sky map announced December 18, 2025. Four complete surveys are planned for the two-year prime mission; the published illustration combines only selected bands.

CHAPTER 08 · THE INTERSTELLAR VISITOR

July 2025–February 4, 2026

The visitor from another star

✦

While the survey was being built, an unexpected object crossed the solar system. On July 1, 2025, the ATLAS survey in Chile reported a comet that had arrived from beyond our solar system. It was named 3I/ATLAS, the third confirmed interstellar object ever discovered.

SPHEREx observed it from August 7 to 15. Its measurements revealed an envelope containing abundant carbon dioxide, along with water and some carbon monoxide. The comet passed its closest point to the Sun in late October. When SPHEREx observed it again in December, its activity had changed dramatically.

On February 4, 2026, NASA reported the December results: the comet had brightened and was releasing a more chemically varied mixture of material, including organic molecules. A plausible explanation was that heat had taken time to penetrate its outer layers and reach buried ices.

This was a real encounter with material from another planetary system. It was also a reminder that a moving object cannot be understood from a single moment. Observations separated by time revealed an evolving physical process. Organic molecules are not proof of life; they are evidence of chemistry worth investigating.

MISSION RECORD

3I/ATLAS was discovered by ATLAS, not SPHEREx. SPHEREx studied its changing coma in August and December 2025; December findings were published in February 2026.

CHAPTER 09 · THE ICE THAT SURVIVES

April 15, 2026

Frozen rivers between the stars

✦

The Milky Way contains places where young stars are being born. Their ultraviolet radiation can be fierce, and yet deep inside the surrounding clouds, frozen molecules survive on tiny dust grains.

In April 2026, researchers reported SPHEREx maps of immense icy regions in our galaxy. Some of these complexes extended across more than 600 light-years. The telescope measured chemical signatures from water ice, carbon dioxide ice and carbon monoxide ice in molecular clouds.

In Cygnus X, one of the galaxy’s active star-forming regions, the maps showed that the densest ice coincided with dense dust. The dust can shield frozen molecules from radiation. This finding supported the picture of ice growing on microscopic dust grains in environments where stars and planets form.

The researchers described these immense frozen regions as interstellar glaciers. The phrase is an analogy: they are not solid sheets of ice drifting through the galaxy. They are diffuse clouds containing grains coated with ice. Understanding them helps astronomers investigate how water and other compounds may be inherited by young planetary systems.

MISSION RECORD

Published April 15, 2026 in The Astrophysical Journal. The mapped regions span more than 600 light-years; the ice is attached to dust grains, not a solid glacier.

CHAPTER 10 · THE DARK WANDERERS

October 8, 2026

The worlds that never became stars

✦

Some objects are born in much the same way as stars, from collapsing clouds of gas, but never acquire enough mass to sustain hydrogen fusion. They are brown dwarfs: dim, slowly cooling bodies whose atmospheres can resemble those of giant planets.

Their faintness makes them difficult to study, especially from the ground, where Earth’s atmosphere absorbs some of the infrared wavelengths astronomers need. SPHEREx’s broad spectral coverage offered a new way of looking at them.

On October 8, 2026, JPL reported results from a study of 37 nearby brown dwarfs. Their spectra revealed signatures of molecules including water, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and methane. The researchers also found differences in their spectra that pointed to complex atmospheric changes as the objects cool.

These observations do not mean that SPHEREx photographed cloud bands on the surfaces of the dwarfs. It measured their light. From that light, scientists can investigate chemistry, clouds and the limitations of atmospheric models. Thousands of additional observations offer opportunities for further study.

MISSION RECORD

The reported study examined 37 nearby brown dwarfs; this is not a claim that all thousands are newly discovered or that the bodies are resolved into detailed planetary photographs.

CHAPTER 11 · THE ARCHIVE MATURES

September 16–November 12, 2026

A second beginning: the data release

✦

The first maps were not the last word. As the spacecraft continued observing, the science team worked on improving the processing that turned raw measurements into calibrated data.

In September 2026, IRSA began releasing Quick Release 3 products. The processing improvements included refinements to the description of how a point source appears in the instrument and to other calibrations important for measuring brightness.

On November 12, 2026, IRSA announced Data Release 1. Observations from Surveys 1 and 2 had been reprocessed with the improved pipeline used for QR3, which contains later survey observations. The release also introduced an all-sky spectral cube, a way to examine the sky through its nominal 102 wavelength channels.

For people who study change, consistent processing is particularly valuable: it helps separate changes in the sky from changes in the instrument’s calibration. The new release was not the end of the mission. It was another beginning, offering a better foundation for questions that had yet to be asked.

MISSION RECORD

DR1 announced November 12, 2026. DR1 and QR3 supersede QR2 for new work. Nominal 102 channels does not imply every channel/position contains valid data; consult the release documentation.

EPILOGUE · THE UNFINISHED STORY

As of November 15, 2026

The next page belongs to everyone

✦

SPHEREx was designed to investigate questions that will not be answered by a single image or a single headline. Its galaxy measurements will help test models of inflation, the proposed rapid expansion of the early universe. Its deep surveys will help investigate the history of light from galaxies. Its ice measurements will reveal more about the material available as planetary systems form.

These are scientific goals, not claims that the mission has already solved those mysteries. The prime mission is planned to produce four all-sky surveys over two years. As of this edition, the observatory is active, and further data products are expected.

This is where sphereXplorer enters the story. A separate educational project can use the public archive to make its measurements accessible: compare genuinely distinct observation dates, inspect individual wavelength bands, and examine candidate changes with their uncertainties and limitations.

The greatest achievement of an open survey is not that it gives everyone the same answer. It is that it gives everyone a chance to ask a better question. From a proposal that once failed to an archive that spans the sky, SPHEREx has made that chance real. The next page is still unwritten.
